 {\small\noindent\textbf{Abstract:}}
 {\par}

  \begin{abstract}
    Ecological networks are widely used to understand biodiversity and
    predict ecosystem responses to environmental change, yet different
    food web representations encode different assumptions about how
    feeding interactions arise. We develop a process-based framework
    that links global metawebs of evolutionarily feasible interactions
    to regional metawebs constrained by spatiotemporal overlap, and
    ultimately to realised food webs shaped by local ecological
    conditions. Five processes govern these transitions: evolutionary
    compatibility and spatiotemporal co-occurrence constrain which
    interactions are possible and available, while species abundance,
    consumer diet choice, and non-trophic interactions influence which
    interactions are realised. We connect these processes to four
    approaches to network construction (empirical, feasibility-based,
    process-based, and structure-based) and derive testable hypotheses
    for evaluating when incorporating ecological processes improves
    predictions beyond those based on co-occurrence or aggregate network
    structure. By distinguishing the assumptions underlying each
    representation, the framework clarifies what different food webs can
    reveal, where their inferences are limited, and how these
    limitations can be tested using independent interaction records,
    trait and distribution data, and repeated observations across sites
    or time. Ultimately, it provides a basis for matching food web
    representations to ecological questions and for testing whether
    explicitly accounting for the processes that generate interactions
    improves our ability to anticipate ecological change.
          \par\bigskip
      \noindent\textbf{Keywords:} food web, network
construction, biodiversity, scale and process, interaction
modelling, trophic network
      \end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

At the heart of biodiversity science are theories linking species
richness, stability, and ecosystem functioning, from the foundational
work of Elton (2001), Lindeman (1942), and MacArthur (1955) to
contemporary questions about biodiversity loss and ecosystem resilience.
Trophic networks are central to these questions because they capture the
feeding interactions underpinning energy flow, community structure, and
ecosystem functioning (Cohen \emph{et al.} 1990; Dunne \emph{et al.}
2002; Martinez \& Dunne 1998; Simmons \emph{et al.} 2021). Documenting
these interactions provides a foundation for understanding biodiversity
and anticipating the consequences of environmental change (Galiana \&
Araújo 2026; Windsor \emph{et al.} 2023).

Yet food webs do more than a description of feeding interactions --
their construction embeds assumptions about the ecological processes
that generate and constrain those interactions. These assumptions have
been discussed in relation to sampling effort (Polis 1991), aggregation
(Martinez 1991), and scale (Martinez \& Dunne 1998; Moulatlet \emph{et
al.} 2024), as well as general interpretation and application (Blüthgen
2010; Blüthgen \& Staab 2024; Dormann 2023). Challenges in collecting
and observing interactions are also well documented (Polis 1991; Pringle
\& Hutchinson 2020; Saberski \emph{et al.} 2024). However, these
discussions have yet to provide a clear process-based account of how the
data and assumptions used to construct a food web shape its
representation, what ecological processes it captures, and which
questions it can answer. Recent work by Gauzens \emph{et al.} (2025) has
synthesised trophic network representations by node resolution and link
type, here we address a complementary question: how do ecological
processes generate different representations, and what can each
representation legitimately tell us?

We propose a process-based hierarchy distinguishing between global
metawebs (representing evolutionarily feasible feeding interactions),
regional metawebs (constrained by spatiotemporal overlap), and realised
food webs (shaped by local ecological processes). The central
contribution is to contrast these representations through the processes
that constrain or realise their links, clarifying how their assumptions
differ, and where their ecological inferences may diverge. This
framework provides a basis for choosing network representations to match
the correct ecological question, and for assessing when incorporating
process information improves ecological prediction.

We focus on trophic networks (food webs), which provide a well-developed
foundation for distinguishing evolutionarily feasible interactions from
those realised through spatiotemporal overlap, abundance, and consumer
diet choice. Although communities are also structured by mutualistic,
competitive, parasitic, and facilitative interactions, we lean on the
extensive work done in the food web space. However, there is potential
for some of the ideas discussed here to transfer to non-trophic
interactions. In this perspective we examine how nodes and edges define
alternative representations (Section~\ref{sec-anatomy}), then consider
the processes (Section~\ref{sec-process}) and construction approaches
(Section~\ref{sec-construct}) that generate them, before discussing how
the framework can guide network choice and ecological inference
(Section~\ref{sec-progress}).

\section{Setting the Scene: The Not So Basics of Nodes and
Edges}\label{sec-anatomy}

Ecological networks represent an `object' from which inferences can be
made and serve multiple purposes. While many aspects of community
structure can be analysed without networks (\emph{e.g.,} trait
distributions or abundance patterns) networks provide a formal framework
for capturing the organisation of interactions among species. These are
used to study emergent properties of the community such as energy flow
(Lindeman 1942; Odum 1968), complexity (Montoya \emph{et al.} 2006), and
stability (Pimm 1984; Schneider \emph{et al.} 2016). They allow
researchers to examine how interactions among species shape communities
(Pecuchet \emph{et al.} 2020), influence their response to environmental
change (Hao \emph{et al.} 2025), and determine community robustness
(Dunne \emph{et al.} 2002; Staniczenko \emph{et al.} 2010).

But a network is not a neutral description of a community. Its structure
depends on what is represented as a node, what counts as an edge, and
the spatial, temporal, and biological scales at which these are defined
(Poisot \emph{et al.} 2016b). These choices affect which features of a
community the network captures and what can be inferred from it
(Moulatlet \emph{et al.} 2024). To understand what different food webs
represent, we therefore begin with how their nodes and edges are
defined.

\subsection{How do we define a node?}\label{how-do-we-define-a-node}

Although nodes are conventionally described as representing species,
they can correspond to a range of taxonomic and non-taxonomic units,
including trophic species (Williams \& Martinez 2000; Yodzis 1982),
feeding guilds (García-Callejas \emph{et al.} 2023), or species-specific
life stages (Clegg \emph{et al.} 2018). These choices reflect
differences in the level and consistency of resolution at which networks
are constructed, rather than aggregation \emph{per se}. Coarser or
mixed-resolution nodes can limit taxon-specific inference (\emph{e.g.,}
whether species \emph{a} consumes species \emph{b}), bias estimates of
degree distributions, particularly generality and vulnerability, and
complicate analyses of extinction or invasion dynamics by obscuring
species identities and the consequences of their loss (Clegg \emph{et
al.} 2018). Yet aggregation can also be appropriate when interactions
among functional or trophic units are more informative than
species-level detail, \emph{e.g.,} analysing extinction patterns across
feeding guilds (Dunhill \emph{et al.} 2024). Node definition is
therefore not merely a methodological choice: it determines which
ecological entities a network represents, which processes can be
captured, and which questions can be addressed (Gauzens \emph{et al.}
2025).

\subsection{What is captured by an edge?}\label{sec-edge}

Understanding how edge assumptions shape ecological process
representation requires distinguishing between potential and realised
links. Potential links represent feasible interactions between species
(Dunne 2006; Pringle 2020), whereas realised links represent
interactions expressed within a system, or the transfer of energy or
materials through feeding (Lindeman 1942; Proulx \emph{et al.} 2005).
Edges can therefore represent different ecological `currencies' (Gauzens
\emph{et al.} 2025). They can also differ in how interactions are
quantified: links may be binary (present or absent), probabilistic
(Banville \emph{et al.} 2025; Poisot \emph{et al.} 2016a), or
represented by continuous measures of interaction strength (Berlow
\emph{et al.} 2004). Edge definition thus depends on both the ecological
currency being represented and how interactions are quantified. These
choices determine the processes a network can capture and the inferences
it can support. For example, a network of feasible interactions can
characterise potential trophic links but cannot, on its own, establish
the magnitude or distribution of energy flow, because its links are not
constrained by the environmental conditions or energetic processes
governing realised interactions.

\subsection{Network representations}\label{sec-representation}

Node and edge definitions, together with the assumptions they encode,
give rise to two well-established types of ecological network:
\textbf{metawebs}, which represent potential interactions within a
species pool (Dunne 2006), and \textbf{realised networks}, which
represent interactions expressed within a particular community, place,
and time. These representations differ not only in spatial and temporal
scope but also in the processes assumed to generate their structure.

A metaweb is fundamentally a list of feasible interactions between
species. It identifies evolutionarily and regionally plausible links,
excludes ecologically impossible (\emph{i.e.,} forbidden) interactions
(Jordano 2016b), and characterises the potential diet breadth of species
(Strydom \emph{et al.} 2023). Feasibility is determined primarily by
trait complementarity, particularly traits associated with feeding, and
can be further constrained by species co-occurrence.

Realised networks, by contrast, are localised in space and time, with
links shaped by co-occurrence, environmental conditions, and consumer
diet choice. Even when represented as binary matrices, their links
reflect interactions that have passed through these ecological filters
and may therefore imply energy transfer. Realised networks are not
simply spatial or temporal subsets of metawebs; they emerge from the
processes governing whether, and to what extent, interactions occur
within a particular community context. Consequently, a metaweb and a
realised network containing the same species may differ substantially in
structure because their links are subject to different constraints
(Strydom \emph{et al.} 2026). Critically, a link absent from a metaweb
represents an interaction deemed infeasible under the assumptions used
to construct it, whereas a link absent from a realised network may
reflect context-dependent ecological constraints, limited observation,
or both.

\section{From Nodes and Edges to Process and
Constraints}\label{sec-process}

The definitions of nodes and edges establish what a food web represents;
the distinction between metawebs and realised networks clarifies which
processes and inferences each representation supports. We frame these
representations as stages in a hierarchy governed by different
processes: evolutionary compatibility, spatiotemporal overlap,
abundance, consumer diet choice, and non-trophic interaction
modification (Figure~\ref{fig-process}). Each transition imposes a
distinct ecological process, making differences between representations
testable in terms of process rather than data format alone. The
framework is not exhaustive, rather it reflects the current body of
theory and evidence that underpins the way in which we construct
networks Section~\ref{sec-construct}. The framework also generates
testable predictions about the transitions between network
representations. In Box 1 we outline how these processes can be
evaluated using comparative, experimental, and time-resolved interaction
data, but treat these as ways to assess the framework rather than as its
primary contribution.

\subsection{Processes that determine the feasibility of an
interaction}\label{sec-process-feasibility}

In order to be feasible between two species they must have some degree
of evolutionary compatibility and need to have some degree of
spatiotemporal overlap. The first reflects trait complementarity; the
second limits potential interactions to species able to encounter one
another. However, it is worth noting that species need no co-occur for
an interaction to be feasible, but rather co-occurrence reflects a means
to identify interactions that \emph{could} occur in the local context.

\textbf{Evolutionary compatibility}

Interaction feasibility is constrained by functional compatibility
between consumers and resources. Traits associated with resource
acquisition and defence determine whether a feeding interaction is
physically and biologically possible, defining the feasible interaction
space represented by a metaweb (Dunne 2006). Evolutionary history and
co-evolution shape these traits, making phylogenetic relationships and
trait complementarity useful predictors of interaction feasibility.

This constraint reflects the evolutionary history of consumers and
resources (Dalla Riva \& Stouffer 2016; Gómez \emph{et al.} 2010;
Rossberg \emph{et al.} 2006; Segar \emph{et al.} 2020), expressed
through trait complementarity between species (Benadi \emph{et al.}
2022). Consumers must possess traits that permit them to acquire and
consume particular resources, while resource traits may enable or
prevent consumption. Interactions lacking this compatibility are defined
as forbidden links (Jordano 2016b), these are interactions that are
physically or biologically impossible and therefore excluded from the
feasible interaction space.

Feasibility need not be viewed as a binary outcome, and it is possible
to represent these interactions as probabilities (Banville \emph{et al.}
2025). For example, Strydom \emph{et al.} (2022) used. probabilities to
express confidence that a feeding interaction is possible. Webs built
based on evolutionary compatibility are aligned with the global metaweb
in Figure~\ref{fig-process} in that they evaluate the presence of an
interaction between species regardless of if they are co-occurring or
not.

\textbf{Spatiotemporal overlap (encounter opportunity)}

Even when consumers and resources are evolutionarily compatible, a
feeding interaction can only occur if they overlap in space and time.
This of course excludes feed on by-products \emph{e.g.,} dung beetles,
however even in these circumstances there is still a degree of
`co-occurrence'. We therefore distinguish spatiotemporal overlap, which
provides the opportunity for species to encounter one another, from
observed co-occurrence patterns, a statistical pattern that can arise
for reasons unrelated to direct interactions (Blanchet \emph{et al.}
2020).

Spatiotemporal overlap restricts the feasible interactions in a global
metaweb to those that could occur within a particular region or
community, providing the transition from global to regional metawebs
(Dansereau \emph{et al.} 2024a). In Figure~\ref{fig-process}, this
transition is illustrated by the metawebs for regions one and two, which
differ in species composition and therefore in the set of feasible
interactions.

Importantly just because species co-occur it does not mean that they
will interact (Poisot \emph{et al.} 2015) and we should not interpret
the structure of regional webs as being an absolute reflection of
network structure (Caron \emph{et al.} 2024). This distinction matters
because co-occurrence treated as evidence of realised feeding blurs the
distinction between regional metawebs and realised food webs,
potentially giving similar network structures very different ecological
interpretations.

\subsection{Processes that realise
interactions}\label{sec-process-realisation}

Within the feasible interaction space (defined by evolutionary
compatibility and spatiotemporal overlap), we identify three processes
(abundance, consumer diet choice, and non-trophic interactions) that
shape which and how strongly links occur, affecting energy transfer
within communities. This is shown Figure~\ref{fig-process}, although
note here it is no longer a question of capturing link compatibility but
rather a quantification of the distributions of links within a given
community. Unlike metawebs, realised webs are the outcome of consumer
behaviour.

\textbf{Abundance}

Abundance provides a baseline modifier of interaction realisation. Under
a null model, consumers feed randomly among feasible resources in
proportion to prey abundance (Stephens \& Krebs 1986; Vázquez \emph{et
al.} 2009). Within the context of a regional metaweb, abundance
influences encounter frequency and the probability of feeding (Banville
\emph{et al.} 2025; Poisot \emph{et al.} 2015), serving as a link
modifier. It can therefore help predict the distribution and strength of
realised links but does not explicitly establish feasibility or capture
selective behaviour and other constraints on feeding.

\textbf{Diet choice}

Here, we use diet choice as an umbrella term for processes derived from
optimal foraging theory (Pyke 1984) and metabolic theory (Brown \emph{et
al.} 2004) that determine the distribution and strength of realised
trophic interactions. Consumers do not necessarily feed in proportion to
resource abundance; they can select resources according to the energetic
return and the cost of acquiring and consuming them (Stephens \& Krebs
1986). Consumer diet choice thus reflects energetic cost--benefit
trade-offs centred on prey profitability and constrained by traits
associated with resource acquisition and consumption (Smith \emph{et
al.} 2021; Wootton \emph{et al.} 2023). These energetic constraints
underpin a range of approaches to constructing realised food webs
(Beckerman \emph{et al.} 2006; Cherif \emph{et al.} 2024; Pawar \emph{et
al.} 2012; Portalier \emph{et al.} 2019).

\textbf{Non-trophic interactions}

Non-trophic interactions are included here not as trophic links, but as
processes that modify the occurrence or strength of trophic interactions
within realised food webs (Buche \emph{et al.} 2024; Ings \emph{et al.}
2009; Kéfi \emph{et al.} 2012, 2015; Miele \emph{et al.} 2019). They
extend the ecological context beyond evolutionary compatibility,
spatiotemporal overlap, and abundance by altering encounter rates,
feeding success, mortality, or access to resources (Golubski \& Abrams
2011; Pilosof \emph{et al.} 2017).

Such effects can arise through direct interactions among consumers,
including predator interference or competition for space, or through
indirect interactions, such as refuge provisioning, habitat
modification, and facilitation, that alter the probability or strength
of feeding interactions (Golubski \& Abrams 2011; Ings \emph{et al.}
2009; Kamaru \emph{et al.} 2024; Staniczenko \emph{et al.} 2010).

With the caveat that trophic and non-trophic processes are not always
cleanly separable. Pollinators, for example, consume floral resources
through trophic interactions while also influencing plant reproduction,
recruitment, and population persistence through non-trophic processes
(Bascompte \& Jordano 2007; Holland \emph{et al.} 2002; Sauve \emph{et
al.} 2016). However in the context presented here we focus on how
non-trophic processes modify trophic network realisation, rather than
extending the framework to mutualistic networks in general.

\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced jigsaw, arc=.35mm, bottomrule=.15mm, bottomtitle=1mm, breakable, colback=white, colbacktitle=quarto-callout-note-color!10!white, colframe=quarto-callout-note-color-frame, coltitle=black, left=2mm, leftrule=.75mm, opacityback=0, opacitybacktitle=0.6, rightrule=.15mm, title=\textcolor{quarto-callout-note-color}{\faInfo}\hspace{0.5em}{Testing the transition from feasible to realised food webs}, titlerule=0mm, toprule=.15mm, toptitle=1mm]

Although recent work from Strydom \emph{et al.} (2026) has shown that
different network representations do indeed result in different
inferences about changes in communities and networks across time it
remains to be seen if these differences can be ascribed to specific
processes. The framework we present here generates predictions that
distinguish it from a purely descriptive classification of network
types. Below we present a series of hypotheses that would allow us to
explicitly test the framework presented Section~\ref{sec-process}, but
also serves as guidance for thinking about the validation of constructed
networks (see Section~\ref{sec-construct})

\textbf{Feasibility constraint.} After accounting for uncertainty in
interaction records, a model that represents evolutionary and trait
compatibility will exclude more biologically impossible links than a
model based only on species occurrence, and its predicted feasible links
will transfer better to independent species combinations. This
hypothesis can be tested by comparing trait- or phylogeny-informed
metaweb predictions with independent feeding records, including both
confirmed interactions and credible forbidden links. Work linking
evolutionary history and trait complementarity to interactions provides
a mechanistic basis for the prediction (Benadi \emph{et al.} 2022; Dalla
Riva \& Stouffer 2016; Gómez \emph{et al.} 2010; Segar \emph{et al.}
2020). However, prediction remains sensitive to the traits measured, and
apparent forbidden links may reflect insufficient observation rather
than true incompatibility. The hypothesis would be weakened if
compatibility-informed models did not improve discrimination of feasible
and forbidden links in independent data.

\textbf{Regional filtering.} For a fixed global metaweb, restricting
links to species with spatiotemporal overlap will reduce the predicted
interaction set, but overlap alone will overpredict realised feeding
relative to observations from the same region for a restricted time
window. This can be tested by comparing regional metawebs with observed
local realised food webs constructed across a limited temporal period.
Here the expectation is that the regional metaweb would overpredict
interactions relative to the local realised web, importantly they would
not recover forbidden links

\textbf{Context-dependent realisation.} Within a regional metaweb,
models that incorporate consumer and resource abundance, and where
relevant consumer diet choice, will predict realised interactions more
accurately than models based on spatiotemporal co-occurrence alone. The
improvement should depend on the extent to which abundance and feeding
preferences constrain interaction realisation, and should be greatest
where these processes explain substantial variation in observed feeding.
This hypothesis can be tested using repeated interaction observations
and abundance data, evaluating predictions against held-out sites or
time periods. Abundance-based models provide a baseline for testing how
much predictive information is gained by moving beyond co-occurrence,
while deviations from abundance-based expectations can reveal the
importance of consumer preferences and other ecological constraints
(Stephens \& Krebs 1986; Vázquez \emph{et al.} 2009; Wootton \emph{et
al.} 2023). The hypothesis would be weakened if abundance- and
diet-informed models failed to improve predictions over
co-occurrence-based models, or if improvements did not generalise to
independent observations.

\textbf{Process-informed prediction.} Models that represent the relevant
processes governing interaction realisation, including non-trophic
effects where these are important, will predict context-specific food
web structure and its responses to environmental change or species
turnover more accurately than models based only on aggregate network
structure. This hypothesis can be tested by comparing process-based and
structure-based models using the same training data and evaluating their
predictions against independent observations, at both the level of
individual interactions and network properties. Non-trophic interactions
may improve predictions where interference, facilitation, or habitat
modification alters encounters or feeding success, but their
contribution should vary among systems (Golubski \& Abrams 2011; Miele
\emph{et al.} 2019). Structure-based models may remain competitive for
questions concerned only with aggregate topology, while process-based
models may perform poorly when relevant mechanisms are inadequately
measured or misspecified. The hypothesis would therefore be supported by
improvements that generalise to held-out conditions and capture
ecological changes not represented in the calibration data.

Across all scenarios, evaluation should distinguish uncertainty in
biological feasibility from uncertainty caused by missing observations.
Non-detection is not equivalent to a forbidden interaction, and
predictive performance should be reported for links and for the
structural or ecological quantities relevant to the stated question.
Where possible, tests should use independent sites, time periods, or
taxa to avoid evaluating a framework against the same evidence used to
construct it, or embed phenomena relative to the specific system.

\end{tcolorbox}

\section{Network construction}\label{sec-construct}

Above we showcase how network representations embed a specific set of
assumptions, thus not only are they representations of biodiversity but
they are also models of ecological systems. This is reflected in the way
we choose to infer (construct) networks. Ideally, every trophic
interaction could be observed directly. In practice, interaction data
are costly to collect, incomplete, and often impossible to obtain across
entire communities (Jordano 2016a, b; Poisot \emph{et al.} 2021). Food
webs must therefore be constructed from empirical observations,
ecological inference, or models of network structure, each of which
embeds different assumptions about how interactions arise.

The choice of construction approach should be dictated by what the
desired network is intended to represent. Is the objective to document
an interaction that occurred, infer whether an interaction is feasible,
predict which interactions will be realised under specified ecological
conditions, or generate a network with specific structural properties?
Each approach imposes distinct constraints on what a network represents
and which inferences it supports. Here, we map these processes to the
different ways in which food webs can be inferred. Focusing on the
information used to assign interactions and the ecological meaning of
the resulting links. We distinguish four broad approaches: empirical,
which records observed interactions; feasibility-based, which infers
potential interactions; process-based, which models how feasible
interactions become realised; and structure-based, which generates
networks according to structural rules. Importantly these approaches map
onto different network representations (Figure~\ref{fig-build}) owing to
the differences in the assumptions they make about how interactions
arise.

These approaches are not mutually exclusive in their implementation.
Process-based models may use feasibility networks as their starting
point, feasibility models may be parameterised using empirical
interaction data, and random network generators may be fitted to
structural properties observed in empirical food webs. The distinction
instead concerns the basis on which links are assigned and the
ecological interpretation given to the resulting network.

This distinction matters because food web topology underpins analyses of
community structure, energy flux, extinction cascades, ecosystem
functioning, and dynamical stability (Danet \emph{et al.} 2024).
Differences in topology can reflect genuine ecological variation among
communities, but can also arise from differences in sampling strategy,
taxonomic resolution, and construction methodology (Brimacombe \emph{et
al.} 2023, 2024b). Understanding how a network was constructed is
therefore essential for interpreting what it represents and which
ecological questions it can legitimately address. Fundamentally, network
construction should be treated as an explicit modelling decision: it
determines what information an edge encodes, which ecological processes
are represented, and what inferences can be drawn.

\subsection{Reconstruction from empirical
evidence}\label{sec-construct-empirical}

Empirical food webs are constructed from observations or
proxies/evidence of realised trophic interactions. However, different
methods capture different aspects of feeding and vary in their temporal
and taxonomic resolution, uncertainty, and the ecological meaning of the
resulting links.

Direct observations record feeding events in the field or laboratory and
provide direct evidence that an interaction occurred under sampled
conditions (Jordano 2016b). However, because interactions are difficult
to monitor continuously across entire communities such observations are
typically incomplete. Consequently, an unobserved interaction should not
necessarily be interpreted as one that cannot occur.

Gut, stomach, and scat analyses identify recently consumed prey, either
through manual identification or DNA metabarcoding. These methods
provide empirical evidence of feeding by capturing snapshots of recent
diet and are subject to biases associated with digestion, detection, and
sampling effort (Pompanon \emph{et al.} 2012; Sousa \emph{et al.} 2019).
The resulting network represents interactions detectable by the method
over its relevant temporal window, rather than necessarily capturing
each consumer's complete diet.

Stable isotope analyses provide evidence of assimilated energy pathways
and trophic position over longer temporal windows, offering indirect
evidence of trophic relationships rather than direct observations of
feeding events (Post 2002). Similarly, molecular approaches, including
environmental DNA and interaction metabarcoding, can reveal trophic
relationships that are otherwise difficult to observe, although the
certainty with which they establish specific feeding interactions
varies.

Although these empirical sources are observations of realised processes
the temporal scale at which this data needs to be collated to ensure
sufficient coverage or sampling effort means that a web constructed from
these data may therefore function more appropriately as metawebs
(Brimacombe \emph{et al.} 2024a). However, empirical metawebs should not
be assumed to be complete (Polis 1991), and inferred interactions may be
needed to supplement observations (see
Section~\ref{sec-construct-feasibility} on gap-filling).

\subsection{Inference of Interactions}\label{sec-construct-feasibility}

Feasibility-based approaches infer trophic interactions from the
properties of consumers and resources, drawing on the evolutionary
compatibility processes described in
Section~\ref{sec-process-feasibility}. Rather than establishing whether
feeding was observed or predicting whether it will occur under specific
ecological conditions, these approaches use ecological rules, species
traits, and phylogenetic relationships to identify potentially feasible
interactions.

Species-specific approaches infer potential interactions between
specific consumer--resource pairs and are therefore conceptually aligned
with metawebs. They typically rely on a combination of the following
assumptions: that ecological rules constrain interaction feasibility
(Morales-Castilla \emph{et al.} 2015); that some of these rules are
conserved across species or phylogenetic lineages (Bramon Mora \emph{et
al.} 2018; Dalla Riva \& Stouffer 2016); and that the properties of
consumers and resources can be used to infer their compatibility.

These approaches are central to constructing first-draft networks for
communities for which interaction data are unavailable (Strydom \emph{et
al.} 2022), supporting the reconstruction of networks in data-poor
regions and for unobservable communities, such as paleo-communities
(Dunhill \emph{et al.} 2024; Fricke \emph{et al.} 2022; Yeakel \emph{et
al.} 2014), or novel future assemblages (Van der Putten \emph{et al.}
2010). These approaches can also help evaluate how species interactions
shape distributions by accounting for both environmental conditions and
biotic relationships (Albouy \emph{et al.} 2014; Gravel \emph{et al.}
2019; Higino \emph{et al.} 2023; Pollock \emph{et al.} 2014).

Feeding rules can either be explicitly specified or inferred.
Expert-based approaches identify feasible interactions using trait
matching (Albouy \emph{et al.} 2019; Roopnarine 2017; Shaw \emph{et al.}
2024), while mechanistic approaches use trait-based relationships
between consumers and resources (Gravel \emph{et al.} 2013; Rohr
\emph{et al.} 2010). Rules can also be inferred using observed
interaction data to train classifiers based on ecological proxies that
are more widely available, such as species traits (Pichler \emph{et al.}
2020). These include generalised linear models (Caron \emph{et al.}
2022), random forests (Llewelyn \emph{et al.} 2023), trait-based
(k)-nearest-neighbour methods (Desjardins-Proulx \emph{et al.} 2017),
and Bayesian models (Cirtwill \emph{et al.} 2019; Eklöf \emph{et al.}
2013). Depending on their formulation and training data, these methods
may estimate interaction feasibility or the probability of observing an
interaction under the conditions represented in the data. These
interpretations should not be treated as equivalent. Interactions can
also be inferred from the structure of known networks. Graph-embedding
uses the structure of a known network to infer the positions of species
in a latent representation, combining network information with
ecological proxies such as phylogenetic relatedness (Strydom \emph{et
al.} 2022, 2023).

These types of approaches can be particularly useful to interpolate
missing interactions within observed networks (Beauchesne \emph{et al.}
2016; Biton \emph{et al.} 2025; Dallas \emph{et al.} 2017; Habedank
\emph{et al.} 2025; Poisot \emph{et al.} 2023; Stock \emph{et al.}
2017). These gap-filling methods infer links from existing interactions
and network structure, sometimes incorporating additional ecological
information. An inferred link, however, is not equivalent to an observed
interaction, rather it represents a prediction supported by the
information and assumptions used in the inference procedure. Thus,
should one use these gap-filling approaches to supplement empirical
observations, it is noted in both the metadata, and ideally codified in
such a way that it captures this uncertainty.

At the core the approaches discussed above are closely aligned with
constructing metawebs (global or regional) as they are (directly or
indirectly) built on the assumption that the evolutionary history of
species determines who they can interact with.

\subsection{Specification based on process}\label{sec-construct-process}

Process-based approaches predict realised food webs by modelling the
ecological processes that determine whether feasible trophic
interactions occur within communities. Rather than inferring feasibility
alone, they operationalise the abundance, diet-choice, and non-trophic
processes introduced in Section~\ref{sec-process-realisation} to predict
which interactions are realised and in (some cases) the resulting
distribution of energy transfer. These networks are used to investigate
energy flux, extinction dynamics, ecosystem functioning, and
relationships between network structure, stability, productivity, and
ecosystem function. They also provide the structural basis for dynamical
models examining secondary extinctions, species invasions, and climate
change.

Because these approaches represent species properties and feeding
processes explicitly, network structure emerges from modelled
consumer--resource dynamics rather than from feasibility rules alone.
The same species assemblage can therefore yield different networks under
different conditions. This detail comes at a cost: models often require
extensive community and environmental data, depend on their behavioural
assumptions, and do not necessarily represent all feasible interactions
beyond the conditions modelled. Note here we explicitly refer to these
approaches as models as they aim to describe the behaviour of the system
(community) as opposed to outright inference.

\subsubsection{Abundance-based models}\label{abundance-based-models}

Abundance-based models provide a simple way to represent the transition
from feasible to realised interactions. Neutral models assume that
consumers feed randomly among feasible resources, with interaction
probabilities related to prey abundance (Canard \emph{et al.} 2014;
Krishna \emph{et al.} 2008). Under this formulation, feasibility defines
the pool of potential interactions, while abundance influences which
interactions are likely to occur.

These models illustrate how two communities can share the same feasible
interaction space yet produce different realised networks because their
species abundances differ. They are not necessarily intended as complete
explanations of trophic interactions; rather, they provide a baseline
process through which feasible links are filtered into
community-specific predictions. They can therefore be combined with
feasibility-based approaches to generate localised food webs (Pomeranz
\emph{et al.} 2019).

\subsubsection{Energetic and behavioural
models}\label{energetic-and-behavioural-models}

A broader class of process-based approaches predicts realised
interactions through energetic and behavioural constraints on feeding.
These models often use consumer--resource body-size relationships as
proxies for the energetic costs and benefits of prey capture, handling,
and consumption. Rather than identifying feasibility alone, they use
energetic constraints to determine which interactions are likely to be
realised.

For example, models developed by Portalier \emph{et al.} (2019) and
Wootton \emph{et al.} (2023) incorporate energetic constraints on
predator--prey relationships. Their resulting links represent predicted
feeding interactions under the assumptions and conditions of the model,
rather than simply identifying biologically compatible species pairs.

Diet-choice models provide a related approach by representing how
consumers allocate feeding effort among available resources. Models such
as the diet breadth (Beckerman \emph{et al.} 2006) and allometric diet
breadth (Petchey \emph{et al.} 2008) models determine interactions
through assumptions about consumer foraging behaviour. Because diet
choice affects both which interactions occur and how feeding is
distributed across resources, these models can generate realised links
and emergent network structure. Their process-based interpretation can
be strengthened by explicitly incorporating trait-based constraints on
feasible interactions (Curtsdotter \emph{et al.} 2019) or complemented
by more explicit representations of nutrient dynamics (Cuff \emph{et
al.} 2024).

These models allow the same species assemblage to generate different
realised networks as abundance, environmental conditions, energetic
constraints, or behavioural rules change.

\subsection{Structure-based construction}\label{sec-construct-random}

Structure-based approaches generate edges according to probabilistic
rules governing network structure, rather than evidence for or
predictions about specific species interactions. An edge in such a
network does not necessarily indicate that two species are ecologically
compatible or that feeding is expected to occur. Instead, it represents
a relationship generated under a model of network structure.

Random and structural models are commonly used to establish null
expectations, provide comparative baselines, and generate synthetic
networks for dynamical modelling. Their primary purpose is generally not
to reconstruct the trophic relationships of a particular community, but
to test whether observed or predicted patterns differ from those
expected under specified structural rules.

Alongside random graph models such as the Erdős--Rényi model (Erdős \&
Rényi 1959), approaches have been developed that take existing networks
and randomise their interactions to generate alternative configurations
while preserving selected structural properties (Banville \emph{et al.}
2023; Bascompte \emph{et al.} 2003; Fortuna \& Bascompte 2006). Although
such models do not directly reconstruct the ecological processes
governing real-world interactions (Bascompte 2007), they provide useful
null hypotheses for evaluating the significance of observed network
structure.

Stochastic food web models commonly impose probabilistic rules governing
properties such as connectance, diet breadth, trophic position, or the
distribution of interactions along a niche axis. The Niche Model, for
example, generates trophic interactions according to species' positions
along a one-dimensional niche axis (Williams \& Martinez 2000). The
Cascade Model similarly generates networks using rules concerning
trophic ordering (Cohen \emph{et al.} 1990), while the Nested Hierarchy
Model incorporates assumptions about nested structure and phylogenetic
constraints (Cattin \emph{et al.} 2004). These models can reproduce
emerging properties of empirical food webs (Stouffer \emph{et al.} 2005)
without explicitly representing the identity-specific mechanisms
responsible for specific feeding interactions.

The resulting networks are best regarded as structural or synthetic
representations that most closely represent realised networks. Their
edges are generated under a network-level model rather than established
through observations or predictions of realised feeding. This makes them
valuable for distinguishing structural expectations from empirical
patterns (Strydom \emph{et al.} 2021b) and for providing inputs to
dynamical models, including allometric trophic networks (Brose \emph{et
al.} 2006; Delmas \emph{et al.} 2017; Schneider \emph{et al.} 2016).

However, the ability of a structural model to reproduce properties of
empirical food webs does not establish that it has recovered the
ecological mechanisms generating those interactions. It demonstrates
instead that the specified structural rules are sufficient to reproduce
the properties being evaluated. The interpretation of such networks
therefore depends on whether the objective is to establish a structural
null expectation, generate a synthetic network for simulation, or
investigate the mechanisms underlying particular trophic relationships.

\subsection{Construction choices shape ecological
inference}\label{construction-choices-shape-ecological-inference}

Food web construction determines not only network structure but also the
ecological meaning of its interactions and the scope of inference it
supports. Empirical links provide evidence of feeding under sampled
conditions; feasibility-based links represent biologically plausible
interactions; process-based links predict interactions under specified
ecological conditions; and structure-based models generate networks
against which observed or predicted patterns can be compared without
necessarily identifying the interactions occurring in a particular
community.

These distinctions are especially important when networks are used to
investigate ecological responses to environmental change. A network
representing potential interactions within a regional species pool can
inform questions about alternative feeding relationships, community
assembly, and the potential for interaction rewiring. However, it cannot
(without additional assumptions) establish which interactions will be
realised at a particular place and time. Conversely, an observed network
provides evidence of interactions detected under specific sampling
conditions but may not capture the full set of feasible interactions or
the distribution of feeding across different environmental contexts.

Network construction should therefore be treated as an explicit
modelling decision (Strydom \emph{et al.} 2026). Its inferential value
depends on the alignment between the assumptions used to construct the
network and the ecological quantity of interest. These assumptions
should be made explicit when networks are constructed, compared, or used
to predict ecological change (Brimacombe \emph{et al.} 2024b). Three
questions should guide their interpretation: What quantity or process is
being inferred? What evidence and assumptions support that inference?
And what independent evidence can be used to evaluate the resulting
network or its predictions?

\section{Making Progress with Networks}\label{sec-progress}

Ecological networks are increasingly used as the first step in a broader
ecosystem-level analysis pipeline (Loreau \& de Mazancourt 2013;
Thuiller \emph{et al.} 2024), and having access to networks is relevant
to unresolved questions about stability--diversity--productivity
relationships, the drivers and consequences of extinctions and
invasions, and the impacts of multiple stressors across ecological
scales (Simmons \emph{et al.} 2021). However, the inferences that
networks support depend on what their links represent, how they were
constructed, and the spatial and temporal scales at which they apply
(Gauzens \emph{et al.} 2025).

We therefore argue that network applications should begin with three
explicit decisions: what do we want to infer, which network
representation can support that inference, and how will the inference be
evaluated? These decisions connect the ecological processes discussed in
Section~\ref{sec-process-feasibility} with the construction approaches
described in Section~\ref{sec-construct} and are expanded on in Box 2.

\subsection{Matching network representations to ecological
questions}\label{matching-network-representations-to-ecological-questions}

The choice of network representation should follow the ecological unit
of interest, rather than simply the availability of a dataset or
analytical method. The distinctions provided in Box 2 serve as a
practical guidance for selecting, constructing, and evaluating networks.
Below we discuss this in more detail for each type of network
reconstruction.

\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced jigsaw, arc=.35mm, bottomrule=.15mm, bottomtitle=1mm, breakable, colback=white, colbacktitle=quarto-callout-note-color!10!white, colframe=quarto-callout-note-color-frame, coltitle=black, left=2mm, leftrule=.75mm, opacityback=0, opacitybacktitle=0.6, rightrule=.15mm, title=\textcolor{quarto-callout-note-color}{\faInfo}\hspace{0.5em}{Question mapping}, titlerule=0mm, toprule=.15mm, toptitle=1mm]

\textbf{Global metawebs}

\emph{Primary question}: Which feeding interactions are biologically
feasible within a broader species pool?

\emph{Use when:} Investigating potential diet breadth, evolutionary
constraints on feeding, novel communities, species introductions, or the
potential for interaction rewiring under future environmental
conditions.

\emph{You need:} A defined species pool and evidence or assumptions
about interaction compatibility, typically based on traits, phylogeny,
or documented interactions. Spatial co-occurrence need not be imposed if
the aim is to represent the broader feasible interaction space.

\emph{It lets you infer:} Which interactions are plausible, which appear
incompatible, and which potential feeding relationships could become
relevant as species distributions or environmental conditions change.

\emph{Check it by:} Testing predictions against independent interaction
records where possible, including evidence for both feasible and
forbidden links. Assess sensitivity to the species pool, trait
definitions, and rules used to infer compatibility.

\emph{Do not infer:} That every feasible interaction occurs, that all
listed interactions can occur simultaneously, or that the network
structure describes energy flow in any specific community.

\textbf{Regional metawebs}

\emph{Primary question:} Which feasible interactions are possible among
species that occur, or are expected to occur, within a region?

\emph{Use when:} Examining potential interactions within a regional
species pool, anticipating community changes following range shifts, or
assessing possible feeding relationships involving introduced or
reintroduced species.

\emph{You need:} A regional species pool, distribution or co-occurrence
information at an appropriate scale, and rules for identifying feasible
feeding relationships.

\emph{It lets you infer:} How regional species availability constrains
the feasible interaction space, and how that space may change as species
distributions shift.

\emph{Check it by:} Comparing predicted species occurrence and feasible
interactions with independent distribution and interaction data. When
comparing regions, separate changes due to species turnover from changes
in interaction constraints.

\emph{Do not infer:} That co-occurring species necessarily interact, or
that differences between regional networks necessarily reflect changes
in feeding behaviour or interaction processes.

\textbf{Realised networks}

\emph{Primary question:} Which interactions occur, and how are they
distributed or expressed, under specific ecological conditions?

\emph{Use when:} Investigating community responses to environmental
change, temporal rewiring, trophic dynamics, or the consequences of
altered interactions for ecosystem functioning.

\emph{You need:} Interaction observations or a predictive model that
represents relevant ecological conditions, such as species abundance,
environmental context, and diet choice. The temporal window and sampling
process should be explicit.

\emph{It lets you infer:} Which interactions are supported under the
sampled or modelled conditions, and (where the data permit) how
interaction frequencies or strengths change through space or time.

\emph{Check it by:} Evaluating predictions against independent
observations, accounting for imperfect detection and sampling effort,
and assessing whether the network captures the properties relevant to
the ecological question.

\emph{Do not infer:} That unobserved links are impossible, or that a
network aggregated across many places or times represents a single
community state.

\end{tcolorbox}

\subsubsection{Global Metawebs}\label{sec-progress_metaweb}

Global metawebs are most appropriate for questions about interaction
feasibility and potential diet breadth (Adhurya \& Park 2025; Mestre \&
Bastazini 2026). They provide a framework for exploring hypothetical or
novel communities under climate change (Hui \& Richardson 2019), species
invasions, reintroductions, and rewilding, including the potential for
species to rewire their interactions (Marjakangas \emph{et al.} 2025).
Because global metawebs represent a broad pool of feasible links, they
can also support investigations of eco-evolutionary dynamics and how
evolutionary history, natural selection, and phenotypic plasticity shape
interaction niches.

However, a global metaweb describes potential interactions, not the
ecological conditions under which they occur. Its usefulness therefore
depends on how well the inferred feasible interaction space represents
biological compatibility and on how that space is translated into a
specific community or ecological scenario. In conservation applications,
for example, a species' potential diet can help identify the resources
it may depend on but cannot establish which resources sustain it in a
particular location (Curtsdotter \emph{et al.} 2011; McDonald-Madden
\emph{et al.} 2016; Rooney \& McCann 2012). Similarly, the presence of a
potential link may indicate an opportunity for rewiring but does not
demonstrate that a consumer will adopt that interaction (Gilljam
\emph{et al.} 2015; Staniczenko \emph{et al.} 2010; Su \emph{et al.}
2024).

\subsubsection{Regional Metawebs}\label{sec-progress_regional}

Regional metawebs constrain the potential interaction space to species
occurring, or expected to occur, within a defined region. This makes
them particularly relevant to management questions involving protected
areas, changing species distributions, and anticipated shifts in
community composition. They can also help refine species distribution
models by incorporating information about biotic associations (Albouy
\emph{et al.} 2014; Araújo \& Luoto 2007; Estrada \& Bodin 2008;
Kissling \emph{et al.} 2012; Pellissier \emph{et al.} 2018).

The key challenge is distinguishing constraints imposed by regional
species availability from constraints on feeding compatibility.
Co-occurrence can restrict which interactions are possible but is not
sufficient evidence that trophic interactions occur. Likewise,
differences in regional metawebs across space or environmental gradients
may arise from species turnover (\emph{e.g.,} (\(\beta\))-diversity),
differences in inferred interaction feasibility, or both. Comparisons
should therefore separate changes in the species pool from changes in
the rules or evidence used to infer interactions.

\subsubsection{Realised networks}\label{realised-networks}

Realised networks are best suited to questions about which interactions
occur under specific ecological conditions and how their expression
shapes community dynamics and ecosystem functioning (Thuiller \emph{et
al.} 2024). They provide the appropriate starting point for
investigating the propagation of multiple stressors (Beauchesne \emph{et
al.} 2021; Crain \emph{et al.} 2008), temperature-related thresholds in
community collapse (O'Gorman \emph{et al.} 2019; Petchey \emph{et al.}
2010), persistence following harvesting or species loss (Allesina \&
Tang 2012; Yodzis 2001), and relationships between stability, diversity,
productivity, and ecosystem function (Rooney \& McCann 2012; Thébault \&
Fontaine 2010). They can also support investigations of metacommunity
dynamics when interactions are considered across multiple local
communities (Gilarranz \emph{et al.} 2017; Gravel \emph{et al.} 2016b).

These inferences depend on how closely the network corresponds to the
community state and process of interest. A binary interaction record
indicates that an interaction was detected or documented, but does not
necessarily quantify its strength, frequency, or contribution to energy
transfer. Furthermore, empirical datasets often accumulate observations
across extended periods or multiple locations, potentially obscuring
temporal and spatial variation in interaction structure (Polis 1991).
The increasing availability of long-term interaction datasets offers new
opportunities to study these dynamics (Danet \emph{et al.} 2024; Wooster
\emph{et al.} 2024), but requires methods that account for sampling
effort, imperfect detection, and the spatiotemporal boundaries that need
to be applied to ensure that one is operating in the `realised space'.

\subsection{Key methodological
challenges}\label{key-methodological-challenges}

Below we highlight some of the key challenges that currently limit the
precision, accuracy, and applied value of ecological network inference.

\textbf{Validating interactions and network structure.} Although ideas
presented in Box 1 are presented as potential means of validation it is
worth being explicit about the need to validate network structure and
pairwise interactions independently. Considerable progress has been made
in evaluating the recovery of pairwise interactions (Abramov \emph{et
al.} 2026; Poisot 2023; Strydom \emph{et al.} 2021a), but there is less
agreement on how to evaluate the recovery of network structure (Allesina
\emph{et al.} 2008; Tylianakis \emph{et al.} 2010). The appropriate
benchmark depends on the intended representation. For metawebs,
evaluation should consider the recovery of both feasible and forbidden
links. For realised networks, it remains important to establish when the
priority is recovering individual interactions, aggregate properties
(\emph{e.g.,} connectance), or both. These objectives are related but
not interchangeable: a model may reproduce a network's aggregate
structure while misidentifying many of its constituent links, or recover
links without accurately representing the structural properties relevant
to the ecological question. For example, Strydom \emph{et al.} (2026)
showcase how structurally similar networks can disagree as to the
specific pairwise interactions.

\textbf{Connecting metawebs to realised networks.} Many approaches for
modelling realised networks do not explicitly incorporate evolutionary
constraints, although exceptions exist (Van De Walle \emph{et al.} 2023;
Wootton \emph{et al.} 2023). Bridging this gap may require ensemble
approaches (Becker \emph{et al.} 2022; Terry \& Lewis 2020) or methods
that downscale metawebs into context-specific networks (\emph{e.g.,}
Roopnarine 2006). Such transitions must account for the ecological
filters that determine which feasible links are expressed, including
species abundance, diet choice, and relevant environmental conditions.
Structural realism alone does not establish that a model captures
realised prey choice or energy flow.

More explicit links between metawebs and realised networks could also
support integration with metacommunity, metaecosystem, and
ecosystem-level theory (Liu \emph{et al.} 2025; Massol \emph{et al.}
2011). Achieving this requires greater clarity about the spatial and
temporal scales at which networks should be constructed and interpreted,
and how those scales relate to the ecological question (Fortin \emph{et
al.} 2021).

\textbf{Making networks useful in conservation and management.} Applied
use remains constrained by difficulties in defining network boundaries,
aligning networks with management units, and translating network metrics
into policy-relevant conclusions (Dansereau \emph{et al.} 2024b).
Addressing these challenges requires stronger links between network
structure and ecosystem function, alongside careful matching of network
representations and analytical methods to management objectives
(O'Connor \emph{et al.} 2025; Pellissier \emph{et al.} 2018). Managers
need to know whether an analysis identifies potential interactions,
predicts interactions under specified conditions, or evaluates
consequences for interaction strength and ecosystem processes. These are
distinct objectives and require different evidence.

Together, these challenges yield three principles: make construction
data and assumptions explicit; align validation with the intended
representation and inference; and model the processes that filter
feasible links into context-dependent interactions. These principles
support more transparent and robust inference across ecological scales.

\section{Concluding Remarks}\label{concluding-remarks}

Ecological networks provide powerful abstractions for understanding
biodiversity and ecosystem functioning, but their inferential value
depends on what their nodes and edges represent, and which ecological
processes underpin their construction. Distinguishing interaction
feasibility from realisation clarifies why global and regional metawebs,
realised networks, and structure-based models support different
questions and should not be treated as interchangeable descriptions of
ecological systems.

The central implication is that network construction should begin with
the intended inference, aligning representation, available evidence, and
evaluation criteria. Assumptions about interaction feasibility,
realisation, and spatial and temporal scales should be explicit, as
should the objectives against which a network is evaluated. Recovering
individual interactions, reproducing network structure, and predicting
ecological outcomes are distinct goals, and success in one does not
guarantee success in another. Potential interactions should not be
mistaken for realised feeding, just as structural similarity should not
be taken as evidence of the ecological mechanisms that generate a
network.

Ultimately, the value of a food web lies not in how closely it resembles
a real-world network, but in whether it captures the ecological
relationships needed to answer a particular question. By making the
transitions from evolutionary feasibility to spatiotemporal availability
and local realisation explicit, our framework provides a basis for
testing when and why different network representations lead to different
conclusions. The challenge now is to move beyond asking whether a
network reproduces observed patterns towards establishing which
processes those patterns reflect, and whether representing those
processes improves predictions of ecological change. Doing so will help
turn food webs from useful descriptions of biodiversity into more
transparent, testable tools for understanding and anticipating how
ecological communities respond to a changing world.
